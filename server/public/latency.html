Low-latency reaction · ConversationRelay

Two problems while the LLM thinks.

A voice agent waits 1–3 s for the LLM's first word. That wait causes two separate problems. The Handler, a step between the caller and the LLM, fixes both.

Problem 1

The gap before the answer

The caller stops speaking and hears silence until the LLM's first token. Callers think the line has dropped, or start talking again.

1–3 s

of silence on a normal turn, longer on a slow one

Part 1 · fill the gap. Say a short acknowledgement ("Okay.") within 400 ms, then play a hold sound until the answer starts.
Problem 2

A very large gap: overlapping answers

On a long gap the caller fills the silence: they add a question, correct themselves or say "hang on". Without the Handler each piece goes to the LLM on its own, so answers overlap, an interrupt kills the first answer and a question is lost.

Part 1 · Problem 1, the gap

Fill the gap: acknowledge at once, then hold

The Handler's first job is to make sure the caller never hears dead air. Step 1: while the LLM thinks, a fast classifier picks one of 101 pre-written acknowledgements ("Okay.", "Good question.") and speaks it within 400 ms. Step 2: if the LLM is still thinking after that, a hold sound plays until the answer starts. The four modes differ only in what picks the acknowledgement.

none · baselineNo acknowledgement and no hold. The caller hears 1–3 s of silence. The Handler needs an acknowledgement, so it does not run.
embedding · local, fastestNearest example phrase, computed on the server. 24 ms, 61% useful, 11.4% wrong among matches.
jev · hosted, most accurateOne choice question over all 102 options. 230 ms, 88% useful, 7.5% wrong among matches. Recommended.
laya · local, not readyA local cross-encoder. Base 31% useful at 216 ms; fine-tuned 69% at 809 ms. Evaluated, not recommended.

How the acknowledgement works

Then the hold sound

The "Okay." covers about half a second. On a slow turn the LLM is still thinking after it, so the Handler plays a typing sound (1 s of silence, then typing) once per turn. The LLM's first token cuts it off, so the two never overlap.

Part 2 · Problem 2, overlapping answers

Build one coherent question before the LLM sees it

The Handler from Part 1 still acknowledges every piece and plays the hold sound. Part 2 adds a second Jev question, coherence: given what the caller said earlier, is the latest piece a question, a statement, filler or a change of mind? The Handler sends only a complete question, waiting up to Question wait for more words, and merges anything the caller says before the LLM's first token. The backend LLM gets one coherent question and answers once.

Embedding sees only the latest piece and often reads questions as statements, which costs a needless wait. Jev sees the earlier pieces too.

The full Handler

Question"Why is my bill so high?" A complete ask, so it goes to the LLM now.
Statement"I got my bill yesterday." Context, and a question may follow, so wait up to Question wait.
Filler"Um, so…" "Hang on." Not finished, so wait up to Question wait, then send what there is.
Changed mind"Actually, no…" Send the whole sentence now. The "actually, no" carries the correction.

Merge and take-back

MergeA new piece that arrives before the LLM's first token is merged into the unanswered question. If the LLM already has it, that stream is stopped and the unanswered message is dropped (retract).
Take-backIf the caller talks over the "Okay." or the hold sound, the interrupt is not passed to the LLM, which would kill the answer. The question is taken back and the next piece merged; if none comes, it is re-sent after max(Question wait, 4 s).
Step asideOnce the LLM's first token is spoken, the Handler stops holding and merging. Interrupts then work as normal barge-in.

Why Laya loses at filler matching

Same job, three architectures. The difference is what each one sees at the moment it picks.

Jev · one shot, remoteAll 102 options and the utterance in one context, one call.Weighs every option against the others, so it can judge intent and say "none of these".
Embedding · retrieval, local404 curated example phrases embedded ahead of time. Only the utterance is embedded at runtime.Cosine nearest neighbour against our own examples: domain knowledge, built in.
Laya · cross-encoder, localModernBERT-large plus a decision head (421M, non-generative). Utterance and options packed into one sequence, each option scored at its own [MASK].Built for a handful of options per question.
Why Laya loses here
  • Too many options. The docs say accuracy degrades past about 20 options. We send 102.
  • Shared token budget. Every packed option shares one 192-token option budget on the English checkpoint, so with about 50 options per group each gets roughly 3 to 4 tokens and is trimmed to fit.
  • Tournament, so errors compound. The server splits 102 options into two groups of 51, then a final round between the winners. Each round sees only part of the list. Smaller groups meant more rounds and about 4x the latency, and were no more accurate in our runs.
  • Confidence is not usable. The shipped calibration for 11+ options is invalid (temperature 0.1006, clamped to 0.5), so its scores don't line up with our 0.6 threshold.
  • Wrong training. Pretrained for general typed decisions (yes/no, pick among a few), not a 101-way intent match. Out of the box it has not seen our examples.
  • Result. 30.7% useful and 64.8% wrong among matches at about 216 ms, against Jev 88.0% / 7.5% at 230 ms and embedding 60.7% / 11.4% at 24 ms.

Takeaway: the right tool depends on option count. Laya fits a few typed decisions per turn, not a 100-way catalogue lookup. For catalogue lookups, retrieval (embedding) or a model that sees every option in one shot (Jev) wins. How Laya reads the options

Laya picks one filler from a list. It reads the caller's phrase and the options together in one read of fixed size, measured in tokens (word pieces of 3 to 4 letters). The options share a fixed part of that read, the option budget, so the more options in one read, the less room each one gets.

Why we couldn't just send fewer options
  • We have 102 options. To send fewer per read, the server splits them into groups and runs a knockout: group winners, then a final.
  • Smaller groups were slower and no better. More groups means more rounds, and each round is another full read: about 4x the latency, no gain in accuracy in our runs.
  • Errors add up. Each round is another chance to knock out the right answer before the final.
  • A bigger option budget works but is slow. That is the fine-tuned model: it can read all 51 options per group, but takes 809 ms.
Why Jev and Embedding don't have this problem
Jev · 230 msA large model that reads all 102 options in full (about 4,300 tokens) in one call. Nothing is cut and there is no knockout.
Embedding · 24 msNothing to fit into a read. Each example phrase is turned into a vector once, at startup. At call time it only compares the caller's phrase against those, so more fillers cost almost nothing.
Laya · 809 ms fine-tunedHas to fit the options into its option budget on every call. With 100+ options it either can't read them or is too slow.
Work per call: why Laya is still slow

A cross-encoder re-reads every option on every call; retrieval does that work once.

Why it improved
  • It can finally read the options. The token budget was sized for a few options. With 102 options, each was cut to about 4 tokens, roughly its ID. Fine-tuning started from the typed-decisions checkpoint (256-token budget, about 5 tokens per option) and raised the budget to 1024, which gives each option about 19 tokens: enough for the ID and most of its description, though not its examples.
  • Trained on our task. 953 caller phrases built from the filler bank, shaped like the live tournament. No eval rows used.
  • Option order shuffled. It learns what an option says, not where it sits.
Why it still isn't shipped
  • Overconfident. Wrong answers score about 0.99, so the confidence threshold can't filter them: 29.5% wrong vs Embedding's 11.4%.
  • Slow. It re-reads 51 options of about 19 tokens on every call: 809 ms, close to the 1000 ms timeout. Embedding computes its vectors once and answers in 24 ms.
  • Costly to change. Every filler edit needs a 76-minute retrain. With Embedding or Jev, it's a config edit.

Takeaway: Fine-tuning fixed what Laya could see, not how it works. A cross-encoder re-reads every option on every call, so with 100+ options, retrieval (Embedding) or one-shot (Jev) wins.

Part 3 takeaway: Jev reads every option in one call, so it judges intent best. Embedding does its heavy work once at startup, so it is fastest. Laya re-reads every option on every call, which suits a handful of options, not 100+.
